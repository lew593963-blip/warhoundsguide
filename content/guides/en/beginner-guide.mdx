Warhounds asks you to think on two connected levels. In the field, you command a mercenary squad through turn-based firefights. Back at base, you run the private military company that recruits, equips, heals, funds, and informs that squad. A strong start comes from treating those layers as one system: every tactical weakness creates a strategic cost, and every base decision should solve a field problem.

The safest beginner plan is simple:

1. Build role coverage before chasing a supposedly perfect character.
2. Reveal lines of fire before committing the squad.
3. Use cover as temporary protection, because the environment can be destroyed.
4. Spend at base to remove the next mission's clearest bottleneck.
5. Treat relationships and operational choices as resources, because the campaign can change around them.

## Understand the campaign loop first

The game's premise puts you in command of a private military company operating in East Africa in 2035. That command role matters. A mission is not an isolated puzzle: injuries, equipment needs, finances, intelligence, allied support, and roster development all feed the next deployment.

Use a three-question loop after every operation:

- **What nearly failed?** Look for a missing role, weak information, poor mobility, exposed firing lane, or sustainment problem.
- **What created the failure?** Separate a tactical mistake from a roster or equipment limitation.
- **What is the cheapest reliable correction?** Training, a different loadout, medical recovery, a new recruit, better intelligence, or simply a different plan may solve the problem without a broad spending spree.

This prevents random upgrading. It also keeps the base layer tied to evidence you can observe in your own campaign.

## Build your first squad around jobs

Warhounds officially names five classes: Assault, Specialist, Sniper, Machine Gunner, and Grenadier. That list confirms roles, but it does not prove a universal best composition. Start by covering jobs instead of forcing a tier list onto the roster.

A balanced deployment needs ways to:

- move or hold the front line;
- gather information and use tactical tools;
- threaten a distant or exposed target;
- control an approach or create sustained pressure;
- break a clustered position or deny an area.

The five class names suggest natural candidates for those jobs, but the right answer depends on the mission, the people available, their equipment, and their condition. Before deployment, ask what your current squad cannot do. If there is no safe answer to a long lane, no way to disrupt a defended position, or no information tool, change the roster or loadout before the operation begins.

## Start each tactical turn with information

Official material emphasizes visible lines of fire, combat scanners, drones, smoke, optical camouflage, high ground, and vertical play. Together, those features support a disciplined turn sequence:

1. **Read the objective and the danger lanes.** Identify where the squad must go and which positions can see that route.
2. **Reveal before committing.** Use sight, scanners, or a drone when available so the first move does not expose the whole team.
3. **Create a safe path.** Smoke, concealment, suppression pressure, or a different angle can be more valuable than a low-quality shot.
4. **Move the minimum necessary force.** Keep another unit able to react if the first move changes the situation.
5. **End with the enemy turn in mind.** Check cover, crossfire, escape routes, and overwatch cones before finishing.

The key beginner habit is delaying commitment until you understand the board. A shot that deals damage now can be worse than a move that preserves two future options.

## Treat cover as temporary

Both official sources emphasize destructible environments. That changes the meaning of a “safe” tile. Cover protects against the current line of fire; it is not a promise that the position will survive the next exchange.

Before settling behind cover, check:

- whether an enemy can flank it;
- whether explosive or sustained fire can break it;
- whether a second piece of cover is reachable;
- whether the position contributes to the objective;
- whether high ground or vertical angles defeat it.

Avoid packing the entire squad around one object. Spacing reduces the damage from a single destroyed position and keeps multiple firing angles available.

## Choose equipment for a problem

The official description confirms a broad selection of weapons and upgradable equipment, but it does not provide an evidence-safe universal weapon ranking. A beginner should choose by job:

| Problem | Loadout question |
| --- | --- |
| Long exposed lane | Who can observe or pressure it without overextending? |
| Hard cover | Who can change the angle or remove the protection? |
| Unknown contact | Which scanner, drone, or cautious move reveals it? |
| Dangerous crossing | Can smoke or concealment reduce the exposure? |
| Enemy approach | Which unit can shape the lane with overwatch? |

## Make the first base decisions solve readiness

Steam explicitly names recruitment, soldier upgrades, equipment purchasing, logistics, finances, medical care, downtime, allied factions, relationships, intelligence, and covert operations. That is a wide command layer. It does not mean every category deserves equal spending immediately.

Use this priority test:

1. **Readiness:** can the intended squad deploy in workable condition?
2. **Coverage:** is a necessary combat or information job missing?
3. **Sustainment:** will medical, financial, or logistics pressure damage the next operation?
4. **Leverage:** can intelligence, an operation, or a relationship create a better opportunity?

Fix the first failing category, then reassess. This is more robust than memorizing an upgrade list because it adapts to injuries, mission demands, and the people available. The [base upgrades and management guide](/base-upgrades) explains the full bottleneck method.

## Treat choices as campaign mechanics

The Steam description states that decisions can change missions, available equipment, and the allies who support you. A dialogue or operational choice can therefore be a strategic input, not flavor text.

Before choosing, consider:

- the immediate mission reward;
- the relationship being strengthened or damaged;
- the equipment or intelligence you may gain or lose;
- the risk created for the current roster;
- whether the choice supports your longer campaign plan.

The exact consequences may be context-specific and patch-sensitive. Save broad claims until the current build and the particular decision support them.

## Beginner mistakes to avoid

### Chasing eliminations before the objective

The safest action is the one that advances the mission while preserving future options. A dramatic flank that leaves the squad unable to respond can be strategically expensive even when it removes one target.

### Assuming cover is permanent

Destructible terrain makes static safety fragile. Keep spacing, alternate positions, and a path out.

### Deploying without a missing-role check

A collection of individually strong mercenaries can still lack information, area control, or a way to handle a distant threat.

### Buying broadly instead of fixing a bottleneck

Base resources should answer the campaign you have, not an imaginary perfect end state. Solve the next limiting factor, then review again.

### Converting marketing language into exact formulas

“True ballistics” and “no fake RNG” are official descriptions, but they do not publish every calculation. Use the observable interface and current build; do not rely on invented percentages or hidden rules.
