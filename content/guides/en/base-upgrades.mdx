The best first base upgrade in Warhounds is the one that removes the next operational bottleneck. The official Steam description confirms recruitment, soldier upgrades, equipment purchasing, logistics, finances, medical care, downtime, allied factions, relationships, intelligence, and covert operations. It does not publish a complete universal build order in the material checked for this guide.

Use this priority ladder instead:

1. **Readiness:** keep a workable squad able to deploy.
2. **Coverage:** fill a missing combat, equipment, or information job.
3. **Sustainment:** stop medical, financial, or logistics pressure from compounding.
4. **Leverage:** invest in intelligence, operations, or relationships that improve future options.

Only move down the ladder when the category above it is stable enough for the next mission.

## What the Warhounds base officially covers

The [Steam listing](https://store.steampowered.com/app/3929470/Warhounds/?l=english) describes the base as the place where you recruit and upgrade soldiers, purchase equipment, manage logistics and finances, oversee medical care and downtime, work with allied factions, manage relationships, gather intelligence, and run covert operations.

The [official site](https://warhounds.game/) frames the player as the PMC commander who selects operations, builds the team, and runs the war room. Together, those sources establish a broad command layer. They do not establish every building name, level, price, prerequisite, or exact return on investment.

That distinction matters: the categories below are official; the ordering is a practical management method.

## Step 1: protect deployment readiness

Start with the simplest question: can a squad appropriate to the next operation deploy in workable condition?

Check:

- injuries or medical pressure;
- missing people in essential roles;
- equipment that is unavailable, inadequate, or concentrated on one soldier;
- insufficient downtime for the intended roster;
- a logistics or financial constraint that prevents the plan.

If readiness fails, solve it before pursuing a broad long-term bonus. A powerful future capability has little value if the current roster cannot field a coherent team.

Medical care and downtime belong here because they protect access to trained personnel. Recruitment belongs here when there is no reliable substitute for an unavailable role. Equipment belongs here when the squad exists but cannot perform the required job.

## Step 2: build role coverage and redundancy

The roster includes five named classes, unique veterans, and trainable recruits. That makes depth a base-management concern, not just a squad-screen concern.

Use the [squad and classes guide](/squad-guide) to identify missing jobs. Then invest so the company has:

- more than one credible way to gather information;
- a replacement for the most critical field role;
- equipment that can move between suitable operators when possible;
- at least one answer to long lanes, hard cover, and contested movement;
- enough roster flexibility to respond to faction and mission differences.

Do not confuse depth with collecting every possible upgrade. Redundancy should protect a real operational dependency.

## Step 3: stabilize logistics and finances

Logistics and finances are explicitly named management systems. Even without an official public formula, their inclusion signals that field power and organizational capacity compete for resources.

Before a discretionary purchase, ask:

1. Does this solve a repeated failure?
2. Can the intended soldier actually deploy and use it?
3. Does it leave enough capacity for medical or mission-critical needs?
4. Is a cheaper change in roster, route, or tactics sufficient?
5. Will the purchase remain useful across more than one narrow situation?

This is a value test, not a claim about hidden prices. It keeps spending tied to observable campaign outcomes.

## Step 4: turn intelligence into leverage

Steam explicitly names intelligence and covert operations. Information has value only when it changes a decision.

Use intelligence to answer questions such as:

- Which roles should deploy?
- Which tactical tool removes the greatest uncertainty?
- Does the operation require speed, control, or endurance?
- What support or relationship could change the risk?
- Is the expected reward worth the medical, equipment, and political exposure?

A covert operation should likewise serve a defined objective. “More operations” is not automatically better management if the cost prevents the company from fielding the right squad.

## Manage relationships as strategic assets

Official material states that choices can change allied support, available gear, and missions. The official site also describes five factions with their own soldiers and tactics.

That makes relationships part of the resource map. Before a decision, consider:

- what immediate benefit it creates;
- which future option it may close;
- whether it improves equipment, intelligence, or support;
- whether the company can absorb the political or operational cost;
- whether it aligns with the type of missions your roster is built to handle.

The exact consequence of a specific choice still requires current-build, context-specific evidence. Do not turn a broad official feature into a guaranteed outcome for every branch.

## A practical base review after each mission

Run the same short audit after every operation:

| Review | Decision |
| --- | --- |
| Who is unavailable? | medical care, downtime, or roster substitution |
| Which job failed? | training, recruitment, equipment, or tactics |
| What was consumed or exposed? | logistics and financial correction |
| What did we learn? | intelligence-driven loadout or operation change |
| Which relationship changed? | update the next mission and support plan |

Documenting the failure matters. If the same bottleneck appears twice, it deserves structural investment. If it happened only because of one poor move, a tactical correction may be better than a base purchase.

## Base-management mistakes to avoid

### Following an unverified universal build order

The checked sources do not publish every facility, prerequisite, and cost. A rigid list can fail when your roster, injuries, relationships, or mission sequence differ.

### Buying equipment without an operator and a job

Every purchase should answer who will use it, for which tactical problem, and whether that person can deploy.

### Ignoring downtime until the roster breaks

Medical care and downtime are official management responsibilities. Treat personnel availability as capacity, not an afterthought.

### Spending on depth before basic readiness

Long-term leverage is useful only when the company can handle the next operation. Keep the ladder in order.

### Treating relationships as flavor

Steam states that choices affect missions, gear, and support. Track these decisions with the same care as equipment spending.

## Frequently asked questions

### What can you manage at the Warhounds base?

Officially named systems include recruitment, soldier upgrades, equipment, logistics, finances, medical care, downtime, allied factions, relationships, intelligence, and covert operations.

### What should I upgrade first in Warhounds?

Fix readiness first, then a missing role, then recurring sustainment pressure, then strategic leverage. This is a transparent planning framework, not an official fixed build order.

### Is there a complete Warhounds base upgrade tree?

The checked official pages confirm the management layer but do not publish the complete tree, costs, prerequisites, or exact returns used by this guide.

### Are relationships important?

Yes. Steam states that choices can affect missions, equipment access, and allied support. Specific branch outcomes need current-build evidence.

### Should I prioritize equipment or recruits?

Choose the option that fixes the actual coverage gap. Equip a capable available operator when the person exists; recruit or develop depth when the role itself is missing or fragile.
